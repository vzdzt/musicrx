Some Rap Songs: Earl Sweatshirt’s Lo-Fi Lament Unraveled


On November 30, 2018, Earl Sweatshirt released Some Rap Songs, his third studio album under Tan Cressida and Columbia Records, a 15-track, 24-minute project largely produced by Earl himself, with contributions from Navy Blue, Adé Hakim, and Standing on the Corner. Born from personal loss and introspection, this lo-fi opus redefined Earl’s artistry. As of March 11, 2025, with its Gold certification, it’s a cult classic—let’s explore its haunting brilliance.


The Messaging and Theme

The blurry, toothy cover—a selfie of Earl—mirrors Some Rap Songs’s raw, unfiltered core: grief, depression, and resilience. After losing his father, Keorapetse Kgositsile, Earl channels pain into tracks like “Shattered Dreams”—“I spent my whole life depressed”—and “Peanut”—“This is not a phase.” “The Mint” reflects on mortality with “Only thing on my mind was death,” while “Playing Possum” honors his parents’ voices. It’s a fragmented diary of vulnerability.


Production and Sound

Earl Sweatshirt, Navy Blue, Adé Hakim, and Standing on the Corner craft Some Rap Songs’s sound, blending lo-fi jazz, warped samples, and vinyl crackle. “Nowhere2go” stutters with off-kilter loops, “Riot!” samples Hugh Masekela’s trumpet, and “Azucar” hums with soulful haze. The 24-minute runtime is tight yet chaotic, balancing brevity with emotional weight.


Features

Some Rap Songs features Navy Blue, Standing on the Corner, and sampled voices of Cheryl Harris and Keorapetse Kgositsile. Navy Blue’s monotone on “The Mint” complements Earl’s despair, while Standing on the Corner’s jazz fusion lifts “Ontheway!”. Parental samples on “Playing Possum” add intimacy, keeping the focus on Earl’s vision.


Tracks to Watch

  • “Shattered Dreams” - A mournful echo that drifts through lo-fi fog, Earl’s voice a cracked mirror of despair.

  • “The Mint” - A jagged pulse of dread and defiance, its warped drop slicing through the haze like a blunt blade.

  • “Azucar” - A soul-drenched reverie, its muted jazz curling around Earl’s dense bars like smoke rings.

  • “Riot!” - A brassy, bittersweet salute, Masekela’s horn soaring over a canvas of familial ghosts.

Previous Works

Earl’s journey to Some Rap Songs began with the raw Earl mixtape (2010), matured with Doris (2013), and darkened with I Don’t Like Shit, I Don’t Go Outside (2015). After a three-year hiatus marked by tragedy, Some Rap Songs shifted his sound to abstract lo-fi, paving the way for Feet of Clay (2019).


Summary

Some Rap Songs is a 24-minute plunge into Earl’s psyche, fusing lo-fi innovation with raw emotion. Its brevity and depth captivate, though its abstraction can challenge listeners. As of March 11, 2025, its Gold status and critical acclaim (Metacritic 86, Pitchfork 8.8) affirm its cult legacy—an essential, introspective gem.


Ratings

Originality (19/20): A stark, lo-fi tapestry that rewrites rap’s edges with a trembling hand.

Production Quality (18/20): A deliberate distortion of sound, its raw beauty blooming in the cracks.

Lyrics and Messaging (19/20): A dense, aching scroll of grief, its weight carried in every fractured line.

Enjoyment Factor (17/20): A quiet storm of resonance for the patient, its depth a riddle for the restless.

Consistency (18/20): A fleeting, unified dirge, its brevity both a gift and a tease.

Overall Rating (91/100): 9.1/10

Some Rap Songs is Earl baring his soul through lo-fi shadows—a fractured, fleeting triumph of grief and grit. It’s not for the faint-hearted, but its raw honesty cuts deep. Turn it low, let the crackle hum, and sink into a world where pain becomes art, a testament to survival’s quiet strength.
