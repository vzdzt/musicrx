Some Rap Songs

Some Rap Songs: Earl Sweatshirt’s Hazy Grief Unspooled


Dropped November 30, 2018 via Tan Cressida and Columbia Records, Earl Sweatshirt’s Some Rap Songs—a 15-track, 24-minute lo-fi lament—peaked at No. 17 on the Billboard 200 with 26,000 first-week units. By March 2025, it’s Platinum with 1.8 billion streams, a raw elegy for Earl’s father, Keorapetse Kgositsile. Self-produced with Navy Blue and Standing on the Corner, its fractured beauty still echoes in 2025’s underground. Veazy’s take: it’s a gut-punch of hazy truth.


The Messaging and Theme


A blurry selfie cloaks Some Rap Songs in green-tinged grief, a 24-minute spiral of loss and survival. After Keorapetse Kgositsile’s 2018 death, Earl unravels in “Shattered Dreams”—“Bad apples fall off the tree”—and “Peanut”—“This is not a phase.” “The Mint” broods “Only thing on my mind was death,” while “Playing Possum” stitches his parents’ voices into a fragile tribute. It’s a lo-fi confessional of depression and defiance, a cracked lens on Earl’s soul. In 2025, X fans call it “grief’s soundtrack,” its rawness still cutting deep.


Production and Sound


Earl, with Navy Blue, Adé Hakim, and Standing on the Corner, forges a lo-fi haze in LA studios. “Nowhere2go” glitches with warped urgency, “Riot!” flips Hugh Masekela’s jazz into a mournful close, and “Azucar” hums with muddy soul. Vinyl crackle and tape hiss cloak its 24 minutes, a stark leap from Doris’s polish. By 2025, its blueprint fuels lo-fi rappers like MIKE and Medhane, a green-threaded echo in the underground.


Features


Some Rap Songs leans light on guests, amplifying its green haze. Navy Blue’s “Hold me down” on “The Mint” deepens the mourning, while Standing on the Corner’s avant-jazz skews “Ontheway!” into jagged brilliance. Cheryl Harris and Keorapetse Kgositsile’s sampled voices in “Playing Possum” anchor Earl’s grief, raw and unfiltered. These touches sharpen his vision, keeping the focus inward amidst the lo-fi storm.

Previous Works


Earl’s Earl (2010) roared with Odd Future chaos, Doris (2013) honed his craft, peaking at No. 5, and I Don’t Like Shit, I Don’t Go Outside (2015) darkened into introspective grit. Some Rap Songs (2018) shrank runtime, embraced lo-fi, and mourned his father, paving the way for Feet of Clay (2019) and Sick! (2022), solidifying his raw evolution.

Legacy & Reception

Its green-hued lo-fi birthed a scene—MIKE, Medhane—and seven years on, it’s a cult classic, raw and unyielding, a testament to grief’s enduring echo in 2025’s underground pulse.
