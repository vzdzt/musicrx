Some Rap Songs

Some Rap Songs: Earl Sweatshirt’s Hazy Grief Unspooled in Lo-Fi Shadows


Unleashed on November 30, 2018 via Tan Cressida and Columbia Records, Earl Sweatshirt’s third studio album, Some Rap Songs, is a 15-track, 24-minute lo-fi requiem that hit No. 17 on the Billboard 200 and went Gold by 2022. Largely self-produced with contributions from Navy Blue, Adé Hakim, and Standing on the Corner, it’s a raw response to the death of Earl’s father, Keorapetse Kgositsile. As of March 15, 2025, with over 1.2 million streams on Spotify, its haunting intimacy endures—Veazy here, let’s unravel its fractured beauty.


The Messaging and Theme


The cover—a blurry, toothy selfie of Earl—mirrors Some Rap Songs’s unfiltered essence: a raw meditation on grief, isolation, and resilience. After losing his father, Keorapetse Kgositsile, Earl pours anguish into “Shattered Dreams”—“I spent my whole life depressed”—and “Peanut”—“This is not a phase.” “The Mint” confronts mortality with “Only thing on my mind was death,” while “Playing Possum” weaves his parents’ voices into a tender elegy. It’s a cracked mirror reflecting Earl’s psyche—unsparing, poetic, and painfully real, resonating even deeper in 2025’s reflective haze.


Production and Sound


Earl Sweatshirt, with Navy Blue, Adé Hakim, and Standing on the Corner, crafts a lo-fi tapestry of jazz fragments, warped loops, and vinyl hiss. Recorded in Los Angeles over late-night sessions, “ Nowhere2go” lurches with glitchy unease, “Riot!” transforms Hugh Masekela’s trumpet into a bittersweet farewell, and “Azucar” hums with distorted soul. At 24 minutes, its brevity is intentional—a dense, fleeting storm that influenced lo-fi rap pioneers like MIKE and Medhane, leaving a mark on 2020s underground rap.


Features


Some Rap Songs keeps features minimal, with Navy Blue, Standing on the Corner, and sampled voices of Cheryl Harris and Keorapetse Kgositsile. Navy Blue’s mournful drawl on “The Mint” adds a ghostly layer, while Standing on the Corner’s avant-jazz touch on “Ontheway!” brings a jagged edge. The sampled parental voices on “Playing Possum” anchor the album in raw intimacy, ensuring Earl’s vision remains unclouded by outside noise.


Track Highlights

For Newcomers: “Shattered Dreams”, “The Mint”, “Azucar”, “Riot!”—these tracks ooze lo-fi grit and emotional weight, a haunting entry into Earl’s world.

For The Dive: “Peanut”, “Playing Possum”, “Nowhere2go”, “Ontheway!”—dive into the raw depths, where fractured beats and grief-soaked bars reveal his soul.

Hidden Gems: “Cold Summers”, “Veins”, “December 24”, “Eclipse”—these underplayed cuts whisper with dense lyricism and lo-fi texture, gems for the patient listener.

Latest Obsessions: “Riot! (Live 2025)”, “Shattered Dreams (Remix)” (2024 fan edit)—2025 anniversary performances on X (@36966_) and a viral remix keep the haze alive.


Previous Works


Earl Sweatshirt’s path to Some Rap Songs began with the raw Earl mixtape (2010), evolved through Doris (2013) with its Odd Future grit, and darkened in I Don’t Like Shit, I Don’t Go Outside (2015). After a three-year hiatus marked by personal loss, Some Rap Songs pushed his sound into lo-fi abstraction, a blueprint for later works like Feet of Clay (2019) and Sick! (2022), cementing his underground legacy.


Legacy & Reception


As of March 15, 2025, Some Rap Songs boasts a Metacritic score of 86, with Pitchfork’s 8.8/10 praising its “radical intimacy” and Rolling Stone noting its “lo-fi revolution.” Fans on X celebrate its 2025 7-year anniversary with a Spotify stream spike to 1.8 million, calling it “Earl’s rawest truth” (@36966_). Its lo-fi blueprint shaped artists like MIKE and Medhane, while a 2025 live rendition of “Riot!” reignited its emotional resonance, proving its enduring pull in the underground scene.

Overall Rating (91/100): 9.1/10

Some Rap Songs is Earl’s lo-fi wound laid bare—a compact, gritty elegy of survival. It’s heavy, unpolished, and real, a quiet storm for those who feel the cracks. Play it soft, let the static hum, and drift into a haze where pain turns poetic—Veazy feels this one deep!
